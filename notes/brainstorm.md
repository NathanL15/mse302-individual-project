## The six for the table

**1. The important notifications get lost among everything else**
- Who: This is pretty much anyone with a phone and a laptop, but it's worse for people who message a lot (students in group chats, parents, anyone on call) and for people with multiple devices that all buzz for the same thing.
- Why: Every app gets to decide how urgent it is, so a message from your mom shows up the same way as a 20% off sale. You either check everything constantly or you mute stuff and miss things that actually mattered.
- Where from: Alumni panel: Amy Tai said "manage notification fatigue" was something worth looking into. Also personal, I have a lot of AI tools and devices set up and every one of them pings me separately.

**2. Scrolling slowly turns into fatigue without you noticing**
- Who: Social media users, mostly Gen Z and students, and really anyone who opens Instagram or TikTok "for a minute" between things.
- Why: Feeds are designed to keep you going, and there isn't really anything in the loop that tells you your break stopped being a break 20 minutes ago, so you end up losing the time and feeling worse after.
- Where from: Alumni panel: Amy Tai brought up "detect doomscroll fatigue". Also personal, this is me between study sessions.

**3. Losing your place after switching between apps**
- Who: Students and anyone doing knowledge work where one task is spread across browser tabs, Discord/Teams threads, docs, a terminal and notes. It's worse if you get interrupted a lot.
- Why: When you switch (someone messages you, or you just wander off) there isn't really anything that saves where you were. Getting back means finding the tabs and the thread again and re-reading to remember what you were doing, and some stuff just never gets picked back up.
- Where from: Personal, from co-op at Shopify and from project work in general. Alumni panel: Charles said engineers own more of the features and docs now, and Milan said AI tools don't have the right context. Both of those come back to context being the thing that's missing.

**4. Private AI assistants aren't practical on normal laptops**
- Who: Regular people who bought an "AI PC" or a new phone and want an assistant that doesn't send their stuff to the cloud, but aren't developers.
- Why: Cloud assistants are the default right now, and everything you tell them ends up leaving your device. Running a model locally on consumer hardware still takes developer-level effort, so the NPU people paid for sits idle and the private option doesn't really exist for them.
- Where from: Personal project: Hexagon CLI, a shell assistant running Qwen3 on the Snapdragon NPU. Alumni panel: Milan on trust and access with AI tools. The Windows Recall backlash also suggests people do care about this.

**5. Real-time transit arrival times are often unreliable**
- Who: People riding transit in mid-size cities (GRT, GO and similar), especially students and people without a car who depend on making a transfer.
- Why: The times on the app tend to drift or the bus just disappears, so people end up leaving early, standing in the cold, or missing the connection, and the people with the fewest other options tend to pay for it the most.
- Where from: Personal, commuting. My earlier brainstorm idea about optimal navigation routes. Alumni panel: Maisie on public-sector capital planning, where the quality of operational data drives what gets decided.

**6. Health data ends up spread across separate systems**
- Who: Patients with a wearable plus more than one provider, anyone with a chronic condition, and people looking after a family member.
- Why: Your watch, your apps, your pharmacy and each clinic portal each have a piece of the record, and nobody really has the whole thing, including you. Trends end up getting missed and you end up repeating your history at every appointment.
- Where from: My previous healthcare research co-op. Alumni panel: Amy Tai on healthcare in grad school, and Thomas and Zach on Open Banking where the customer owns their data. It's the same idea applied to health data.
